# Rebound — the long game

This document sets out the proposed overall structure: layers, eras, a progression map with target hours, a content
inventory (counts now against counts needed), and the first hour redrawn beat by beat. The proposals it refers to (P-n)
are in `design/proposals.md`.

## Pillars (the test for every addition)

1. **Every system names its rebound.** If a mechanic cannot say which rebound it expresses, it is cut.
2. **Every layer changes how you play, not just how fast.** A new layer brings a new kind of decision.
3. **Efficiency is easy and restraint is built.** Growth automates itself early; restraint needs Institutions, Mandate
   and patience. The game is about the difference.
4. **The lesson is learnt by failing first, then rewarded.** Backfire pays best in the first two runs. Decoupling pays
   best from the third onward and for ever after.
5. **History is right or it is not in.** Real names and dates appear only where they are sure. The cast is fictional and
   no living people appear.

## The shape: three acts, three layers

| | Act I: The Coal Question | Act II: The Great Acceleration | Act III: The Attention Age |
|---|---|---|---|
| Setting | One nation, 1780–1914 | The world, 1909–2010 | The planet, 2000–2100 |
| Eras | 1 Steam · 2 Iron & Steel · 3 Light & Current | 4 Plenty · 5 Motion · 6 Comfort · 7 Freight & Flight | 8 Compute · 9 Code · 10 Transition |
| Prestige layer | **Reckoning** → Hindsight | **Treaty** → Precedent | **Long Now** → Legacy |
| What you manage | A number (R against η) | A system of regions (leakage) | Several boundaries at once (problem-shifting) |
| Restraint loop | Budget, Settlement, Commons | Articles, supply-side treaties | Retiring fossil eras, boundary trade-offs |
| Ends with | Act I settled → the first Treaty | World within budget → the Long Now | All eras settled, Transition displacing → credits, then the post-game |

Each act behaves like Universal Paperclips' act changes: the screen, the gauges and the main verb change.

- **Act I:** "buy cards, found uses, hold a budget".
- **Act II:** "set policy for a region among rivals".
- **Act III:** "trade off boundaries and retire the old world".

### Layer 0: inside a run

Moment to moment, the player:

- buys cards (with forks);
- founds applications;
- answers dispatches;
- raises levers;
- buys Commons;
- **draws and defends budgets**;
- **settles** eras.

A run is 25–45 minutes at first and stretches to 2–4 hours (with idle) by the late game, because Settlement removes the
"oldest era kills you" wall.

### Layer 1: Reckoning (Hindsight)

As v1, with three changes:

- a new formula (wellbeing per ton, decades within budget, coal left unburnt, Settlement);
- Institutions as a **loadout** with slots;
- Know-how as **trees with forks** and visible rebound side-effects.

Histories (run modifiers) are chosen at the Reckoning from hour 10.

### Layer 2: Treaty (Precedent)

This layer unlocks once Steam, Iron and Light have each been settled at least once.

**Reset.** Hindsight, Know-how and the Institution loadout reset. Institutions you have unlocked stay unlocked (you
re-buy their levels). Achievements, histories, records and the Archive are kept.

**New.** The World:

- you pick a region;
- 3 other regions appear, rising to 6;
- oil, carbon and later minerals become global;
- Articles change the rules of the game itself.

Act II eras open only inside the World.

**Why the reset is worth it.** Precedent buys Articles that make Act I trivial to settle (for example, *Standing Commission*
starts Steam settled at the end of the first decade). The first hour of each Treaty replays Act I in ten minutes, then
moves on.

### Layer 3: Long Now (Legacy)

This layer unlocks once the World has held a global budget for 50 years with Freight & Flight open.

**Reset.** Treaties and Articles reset. Each Long Now carries forward **one planetary boundary as a permanent
constraint** of the player's choice, along with its *Legacy* bonus. Planetary boundaries (P18) replace single-era
Pressure. Act III eras open.

**The true ending.** Every era is settled, Transition is displacing fossil supply, and the World has held the
boundaries for 50 years. The credits roll and the game continues into the post-game.

### Post-game: the Long Now ladder

- **The history ladder** (P19): 30 histories that stack.
- **The weekly Almanac.**
- **Records.**
- **The Archive.**
- **The Steady Century** epilogue.

## The ten eras

Each era lists its mechanic of its own, its resource and Pressure, six applications, its efficiency chain, its forks, and
its rebounding lever. Dates are given only where they are sure. Starred names (*) need a check by the content author
before they ship.

### Act I: The Coal Question

**1. Steam (1780–1870): motive power, coal.**

- **Mechanic:** seams. The reserve is a ladder of seams of rising cost; cheap pumping power opens the next. The player
  decides whether to sink each one.
- **Applications:** Mine pumping · Winding engines · Cotton mills · Railways · Steamships · Steam ploughing (Fowler, late
  1850s).
- **Chain:** Smeaton's improvements → Separate condenser (Watt, patent 1769) → Rotative engine (sun-and-planet, 1781) →
  High-pressure steam (Trevithick, c. 1800) → Cornish engine → Compound engine → Triple expansion (1880s) → Steam turbine
  (Parsons, 1884).
- **Capital cards:** Screw-cutting lathe (Maudslay) · Lean's Engine Reporter (1811; published engine "duty" figures, an
  early efficiency league table) · Limited Liability Act (1855).
- **Forks:**
  - Boulton & Watt's premium (a third of the coal saved) against pirate engines.
  - Parliamentary trains (1844) against first-class lines.
- **Research:** The Coal Question (Jevons, 1865), which is the reveal.
- **Rebounding lever:** *Coal duty with exemptions for "productive use"*. The exempt applications grow.
- **Seams:** Shallow coal · Main seam · Deep seams · Concealed coalfield · Undersea workings.

**2. Iron & Steel (1828–1914): tons of iron and steel; coke, drawn from Steam's coal reserve (the first shared stock).**

- **Mechanic:** inputs. Steel output lowers capital cost `c` in Steam, Light and Motion, so cheap steel means more
  engines. This is the embodied rebound. Jevons' own illustration of the paradox was iron smelting: the hot blast cut coal
  per ton of pig iron to about a third, yet the trade's total coal use rose. (Verify the exact quotation against *The Coal
  Question*, ch. VII, before quoting it.)
- **Applications:** Rails · Ship plate · Bridges & girders · Machine tools · Tinplate & cans · Barbed wire (1870s; fences
  in the prairie, and so adds land to Plenty later).
- **Chain:** Hot blast (Neilson, 1828) → Wet puddling* → Bessemer converter (1856) → Siemens–Martin open hearth (1860s) →
  Gilchrist–Thomas basic process (1878) → By-product coke ovens.
- **Pressure:** Slag & smoke. Crisis: *The Black Country*.
- **Fork:** Sheffield crucible quality (high margin, low volume) against Bessemer tonnage.
- **Rebounding lever:** *Tariff protection*. It keeps the price up, but foreign steel arrives in the World layer.

**3. Light & Current (1870–1950): lumens; town gas, then current.**

- **Mechanic:** load-building. Income depends on capacity utilisation. Capacity is built for the evening peak, and the
  player is tempted to sell the trough with off-peak tariffs, trams and appliances, as Samuel Insull did at Chicago
  Edison. Gas and electric are rival supply technologies with different resources.
- **Applications:** Street lamps · Lit homes · Night shifts · Shop signs · Electric trams · Picture palaces.
- **Chain:** Batswing burner → Welsbach mantle (1880s–90s) → Carbon filament (Swan and Edison, c. 1880) → Drawn tungsten
  (Coolidge, c. 1910) → Gas-filled lamp (Langmuir, 1913) → Fluorescent tube (late 1930s).
- **Revival, when Compute opens:** the LED. Blue LED, Nobel Prize 2014. Lit outdoor area grew about 2.2% a year in 2012–16
  (Kyba et al., 2017).
- **Capital cards:** Penny-in-the-slot meter · Central Electricity Board (1926).
- **Fork:** Gas works against power station.
- **Rebounding lever:** *Off-peak tariff*. It flattens the peak and creates new night demand (the storage heater).

### Act II: The Great Acceleration (in the World)

**4. Plenty (1909–2000): calories; groundwater (stock) and land (bounded).**

- **Mechanic:** land and return flows.
  - Yield upgrades spare land when food demand is saturated (Borlaug's hypothesis) and clear more when it is elastic
    (Jevons). The player sees which regime each application is in.
  - Irrigation efficiency cuts return flows to the aquifer: the paradox of irrigation efficiency (Grafton et al.,
    *Science*, 2018).
- **Applications:** Bread grain · Fertilised fields · Meat & dairy · Irrigated orchards · Air-freighted produce (feeds
  Freight) · Biofuels (late; problem-shifting).
- **Chain:** Haber–Bosch (first plant 1913) → Tractors (Fordson, 1917) → Hybrid maize (1930s) → Centre-pivot irrigation
  (Zybach, patented 1952) → Dwarf wheat (Borlaug, 1960s) → Drip irrigation (Blass; Netafim, 1965) → Precision agriculture
  (GPS, 1990s).
- **Pressure:** Nitrogen. Crises: *The Dust Bowl* (a land crisis, 1930s) and *The Dead Zone*.
- **Rebounding lever:** *Drip irrigation subsidy*. Water applied per hectare falls, while total consumption and
  groundwater pumping rise (the Rajasthan case in Grafton et al.).

**5. Motion (1950–2000): vehicle-miles; oil (global in the World).**

- **Mechanic:** a travel-time budget and sprawl.
  - Speed and efficiency upgrades buy distance, not time: the Marchetti constant, roughly an hour of travel a day.
  - Road capacity fills: Downs (1962), the Downs–Thomson paradox, and Duranton & Turner's "fundamental law of road
    congestion" (2011).
  - Sprawl is a stock that decays over decades and sets minimum demand (v1's lock-in made visible).
  - Oil shocks are World events (1973, 1979).
- **Applications:** Delivery vans · Commuting · Suburbs · Out-of-town shopping · The school run · Motorway haulage.
- **Chain:** Radial tyres (Michelin, 1946) → Fuel injection → Lean burn → Wind-tunnel bodywork → Hybrid drive (late
  1990s).
- **Capital cards:** Motorway programme (M1, 1959) · Hire purchase.
- **Rebounding levers:**
  - *Fuel economy standard* (CAFE, 1975). It spawns the *Light truck loophole* application: most technical gains went
    into weight and power (Knittel, 2011).
  - *Road building to relieve congestion* (induced traffic; SACTRA, 1994).

**6. Comfort (1920–2000): household services; current, drawn from Light & Current.**

- **Mechanic:** ratcheting standards and the time rebound. Saturation rises with use and does not fall back (Shove,
  *Comfort, Cleanliness and Convenience*, 2003). Appliances free time, and the time is re-spent on travel and on higher
  standards (Cowan, *More Work for Mother*, 1983).
- **Applications:** Refrigerators · Washing machines · Central heating · Frozen food · Air conditioning · The second fridge.
- **Chain:** Hermetic compressor (late 1920s) → Freon refrigerant (c. 1930) → Loft insulation → Thermostatic controls →
  Energy labels (EU, 1990s) → Inverter compressors.
- **Pressure:** Ozone, then heat. Crisis: *The Ozone Hole* (Farman, Gardiner and Shanklin, 1985).
- **The good-news lever:** *Montreal Protocol* (1987). It works, cleanly, which is the game's proof that some things do.
- **Rebounding lever:** *Scrappage rebate*. The old fridge goes to the garage.

**7. Freight & Flight (1956–2010): tonne-km and passenger-km; jet fuel and bunker fuel.**

- **Mechanic:** two ledgers. Territorial and consumption accounts are shown side by side. Cheap freight lets production
  move abroad: your gauge falls, the world's rises. Border carbon adjustment (an Article) is the counter.
- **Applications:** Container trade · Package holidays (Horizon Holidays, Corsica, 1950) · Air freight · Fast fashion ·
  Cruise ships · Long-haul weekends.
- **Chain:** The standard container (McLean, *Ideal X*, 1956; ISO standards, late 1960s) → Jet airliner (707, 1958) →
  High-bypass turbofan (747, 1970) → Very large container ships (2000s) → Composite airframes (787, 2011) → Slow steaming
  (a fork: η up, speed down).
- **Capital card:** Airline deregulation (US, 1978).
- **Rebounding lever:** *Frequent flyer levy with a free first flight*. It shifts flights rather than cutting them.

### Act III: The Attention Age (in the Long Now)

**8. Compute (2000–2025): operations; power, water and chips.**

- **Mechanic:** autopilot and free at the point of use.
  - η rises on its own (Koomey: computations per kWh doubling roughly every 1.6 years over 1946–2009).
  - Ad-funded applications show the consumer a price of zero and grow straight to saturation.
  - *Proof-of-work* is an application whose resource use is set by its reward, so efficiency cannot touch it.
  - Wirth's law (1995): software slows faster than hardware speeds up. This is a drag on η that Code inherits.
- **Applications:** Spreadsheets · The web · Streaming · Cloud storage ("keep everything") · Proof-of-work · Model training.
- **Chain:** as v1.
- **Revivals into older eras:** LED (Light), smart thermostats (Comfort).
- **Rebounding lever:** *Efficient data-centre certification*. Power usage effectiveness falls and the build-out grows.

**9. Code (2025–): working features; human attention (flow).**

- **Mechanic:** as v1 (the code stock, upkeep, deletion, review), plus Lehman's laws of software evolution: a system in use
  keeps growing and grows more complex unless work is spent to stop it.
- **New application:** *Inference everywhere*. The cost of a fixed level of AI capability fell more than 280-fold between
  November 2022 and October 2024 (Stanford AI Index, 2025); it draws on Compute.
- **Applications:** Internal tools · Throwaway personal apps · Legacy rewrites · Chatbots on everything · Inference
  everywhere · Agents writing for agents.
- **Rebounding lever:** *Agent reviewers*. Review gets cheaper, so more is shipped.

**10. Transition (2020–2100): clean energy; critical minerals (stock).**

- **Mechanic:** displacement. New clean supply adds to fossil supply rather than replacing it, unless the fossil eras are
  capped. York (2012) estimated that each unit of non-fossil energy displaced less than a quarter of a unit of fossil
  energy. Mining for copper and lithium brings back Steam's pumping problem: cheaper extraction finds more to extract.
- **Applications:** Solar farms · Electric cars (also a Motion revival) · Heat pumps · Grid batteries · Green hydrogen ·
  Desalination.
- **Chain:** Mass-produced PV → LFP batteries → … (future cards are marked as forecasts in their blurbs).
- **Retirement levers** (supply-side): *Coal phase-out* (Britain's last coal power station, Ratcliffe-on-Soar, closed in
  2024) and *Keep it in the ground*.
- **Ending condition:** every fossil era is retired or settled while wellbeing holds.

### The Commons (from the third Reckoning, all acts)

| Act | Projects |
|---|---|
| I | Public baths and wash-houses (1846 Act) · Free public libraries (1850 Act) · Parks · Allotments · Mechanics' institutes · The eight-hour day |
| II | Public health service · Green belt · Rail electrification · Repair shops · The shorter week (Keynes, 1930) |
| III | Commons broadband · Libraries of things · Right to repair · The four-day week · Re-wilding |

### Snapshots

- **Hour 1.** "Do I buy the condenser or found the mills first?" The player has just seen coal use triple behind a −40%
  card and called their first Reckoning. The decision: two Institution slots, five candidates.
- **Hour 5.** Running Steam inside a declining budget with a cap, choosing Bessemer tonnage against Sheffield quality, and
  watching cheap steel push Steam's coal up through the capital channel. Saving to settle Steam.
- **Hour 20.** In the World with one rival. The player's cap in the Workshop lowered the world coal price and the rival's
  use jumped (the green paradox). Deciding between a supply-side Article and border adjustment. Plenty is open: a drip
  subsidy has just emptied an aquifer.
- **Hour 60.** Six regions; Freight's two ledgers show their territorial use falling 30% while consumption-based use is up
  40%. Choosing which planetary boundary to carry into the Long Now. Standing orders run Act I unattended.
- **Hour 150.** Post-game: rung 14 of the history ladder ("Coal-rich + Laissez-faire + Moore forever"), chasing a record
  for wellbeing per ton in Compute, and reading the Archive entry on Insull.

## Idle, automation and the phone

- **Session shape.** By hour 5 a phone session is about 2 minutes: read the "While you were away" ledger (what grew, what
  lapsed, which dispatch expired with its default), answer one or two dispatches, adjust a budget, leave.
- **Offline cap.** 4 h at first, 12 h via *Civil Service* (Act I), 24 h via a Treaty Article. `fastForward` stays coarse
  (5 s steps) and runs standing orders, so offline play is faithful.
- **Automation ladder** (P11). Each step names its rebound:

  | When | Automation | Note |
  |---|---|---|
  | Run 2 | The Market | Efficiency and founding automate themselves |
  | Hour 5 | Civil Service | Holds a budget with chosen levers |
  | Hour 8 | Treasury rules | Commons spending |
  | Hour 16 | Policy scripts for rival regions | |
  | Hour 60 | Standing Act I | Settled eras need no attention |

- **Quality of life.** Buy-max and "buy to fork" buttons. Settled eras collapse to one row. No more than one new gauge per
  era. Thumb-reach controls. Every number has a formula sheet (v1 brief). No push notifications (the page cannot, and the
  tone would not).
- **Performance budget.** At most 4 eras fully simulated at 10 Hz; settled eras are constants; rival regions use the
  cheap script solve every second.

### Keeping volume from turning into filler

- **An era kit.** Every era ships the same checklist: 6 applications, 8 efficiency cards, 3 capital cards, 4 forks, 2
  prospects or research, 5 levers (≥ 1 rebounding), 3 Commons, 18 dispatches, 60 headlines, 8 achievement templates
  instantiated, Archive notes for all of it, and **exactly one new mechanic with one new widget**. Anything beyond the
  kit must justify itself against the pillars.
- **Numbers are generated, words are not.** Cost curves, lever level tables and saturation ladders come from small helper
  functions in content (as v1 already does for labels). Each era is tuned by about 8 knobs, not 200 hand-typed numbers.
- **Templates only for commentary on state.** Ambient headlines and achievement families are templated. Dispatches,
  blurbs, the cast and the Archive are hand-written.
- **A content lint test** (`bun test`). It checks:
  - ids are unique;
  - every `Cond` and `requires` resolves;
  - every lever has a `catch`;
  - every era has a rebounding lever and one mechanic field;
  - headlines are ≤ 70 characters;
  - no banned Americanisms (a small list);
  - every dispatch choice has a cost;
  - every Archive note cites a source string.
