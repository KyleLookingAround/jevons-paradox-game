# Energy and its classic services, c. 1700–2025: a source book for Rebound

Compiled 18 November 2026 for the content authors. Every figure has a source, listed at the end. How to read the marks:

- **[V]**: checked against a primary source or a scholarly source during this pass.
- **[S]**: from a reputable secondary source (encyclopaedia, museum, trade body); likely right, but not checked against the original.
- **[approx]**: an order-of-magnitude or commonly quoted figure; good enough for flavour text, not for a card that leans on the number.
- **[unverified]**: commonly repeated but not confirmed. Use it only as rumour, legend, or a broadsheet "it is said".
- **[derived]**: my own arithmetic from verified inputs; the method is shown.

Where sources disagree I give the range rather than pick one. A dry joke needs a true number behind it.

---

## 0. Units and conversions authors will need

- **Duty** (steam pumping engines): pounds of water lifted one foot per bushel of coal burned. The bushel was not standard. A Newcastle bushel was about 84 lb, a Cornish bushel 94 lb, and later reports (Jevons's 1859 rows, for instance) work per 112 lb (one hundredweight). Always say which.
- **Duty to coal per horsepower-hour** [derived]: 1 hp-hour = 1,980,000 ft-lb, so lb of coal per hp-hour = 1,980,000 × (lb per bushel) ÷ duty. For an 84 lb bushel that is about 166 million ÷ duty (duty in millions). Check: Smeaton's 9.45 million gives 17.6 lb/hp-h, which matches the figure secondary sources quote for Smeaton. The conversion holds.
- **Light**: lumens (lm) are a flow of light; lumen-hours (lm·h) are the service. Nordhaus prices light in labour-hours per 1,000 lumen-hours; Fouquet & Pearson in £ (2000 prices) per million lumen-hours.
- **Rebound**: the share of the expected engineering saving that is not realised. 0% means the full saving arrives; 100% means no saving; over 100% is **backfire** (Jevons's paradox proper).

The shape for the game: output rose roughly 100-fold from 1700 to 1913 while the coal cost of a horsepower-hour fell roughly 10-fold. Then Britain left coal entirely, in the same decade that the world burned more of it than ever. **Leakage at planetary scale** (see Ironies).

Jevons's iron example (direct and indirect rebound in one sentence) [V]: the "reduction of the consumption of coal, per ton of iron, to less than one-third of its former amount, has been followed, in Scotland, by a ten-fold total consumption", together with "the indirect effect of cheap iron in accelerating other coal-consuming branches of industry". This is the 1830–1863 window.

### 1.3 Applications opened by cheaper power (in order of the price threshold)

- **Mine drainage** (1712 onwards): the first and only use Newcomen could afford, since he burned unsaleable small coal at the pithead. Cheap pumping opened deeper seams, so efficiency *increased the reserve*; this already exists as the Era 1 twist.
- **Cornish copper and tin mines** (1770s–): where coal had to be shipped in, Watt's economy was decisive. Cornwall is where duty was measured, published and competed over.
- **Blast-furnace blowing** (1776): one of the first two Boulton & Watt engines blew John Wilkinson's furnace [approx].
- **Rotary mills** (1780s–): cotton spinning, brewing, flour (the Albion Mill, London, 1786, which burned down in 1791 [approx]).
- **Railways**: Stockton & Darlington 1825; Liverpool & Manchester opened 15 Sep 1830, when William Huskisson MP was killed by *Rocket* on opening day [approx; standard dates]. Railway Mania, mid-1840s.
- **Steamships**: the compound engine (1860s) made long-haul cargo steamers pay. The **Suez Canal** opened 17 Nov 1869 [V] and cut London–Bombay from 10,667 to 6,274 miles (41.2%) [S]. It was "entirely unsuitable for sailing ships" [S], so it was effectively a steam-only route. Steam tonnage from Asia rose ~178% in the four years after it opened [S, thesis].
- **Electric generation** (1880s–): the turbine made the steam engine the servant of the next era.

### 1.4 Pressures and crises

- **1306/1307**: Edward I proclaims against burning sea-coal in London kilns [S; sources split on the year]. Stories of executions for burning coal are **[unverified]**: treat them as legend.
- **1661**: John Evelyn's *Fumifugium: or the Inconveniencie of the Aer and Smoak of London Dissipated*, addressed to Charles II, rails against the "Hellish and dismall Cloud of Sea-Coale" [approx; standard quotation, wording varies by edition].
- **1853**: Smoke Nuisance Abatement (Metropolis) Act, under Palmerston as Home Secretary [approx].
- **Victorian fogs** (1873, 1880, 1891): notable London fog mortality episodes are often cited [unverified; no death tolls confirmed this pass, so do not use numbers].
- **1952, 5–9 December, the Great Smog**: estimates run from about 4,000 deaths (contemporary and GLA figure) to 12,000 (later re-analysis attributing about 8,000 more deaths in the following months) [S, Britannica / GLA / Davis, Bell & Fletcher 2002]. Prize cattle reportedly asphyxiated at the Smithfield show [S]. It produced the **Clean Air Act 1956**.
- **1962, December smog**: 100–180 excess deaths (GLA) to ~750 (Britannica) [S; disputed]. The Clean Air Act had not yet bitten.
- **Donora, Pennsylvania, October 1948** and the **Meuse Valley, Belgium, December 1930**: classic industrial smog deaths [approx; tolls not verified this pass].
- **Coal and the Royal Navy**: the turbine, then oil-firing, tied naval power to fuel supply. Era-transition flavour; details not verified here.

### 1.5 Restraint: what was tried, and what happened

- **London coal duties** (lever: a tax that funded a city). The City levied dues on coal entering the port from medieval times; after the Great Fire of 1666 Parliament added duties to pay for rebuilding (including St Paul's and the City churches, per standard accounts [approx]). The government duty ended in 1831; the City's ran on until 1889/1890 [S]. About 200 boundary "coal-tax posts" still ring London [S]. *Lever design*: a resource tax that never cut use, because demand was inelastic and the revenue was the point. "Revenue lever": raises Capital or Mandate, cuts nothing.
- **Jevons's own proposals**: he rejected restrictive legislation ("restrictive legislation may mar but cannot mend the natural course of industrial development" [V]) and argued for paying down the National Debt while coal was cheap. He discussed coal taxes and warned "A small tax on the ton of coal would be a heavy tax on the ton of iron" [V]. Gladstone's 1866 Budget took up the debt-reduction idea, which went nowhere [S].
- **Royal Commission on Coal Supplies**: appointed 1866, reported 1871 in 22 volumes, chaired by the Duke of Argyll [S]. It concluded there was "an abundant supply of coal" for the present [S] and estimated around 146 billion tons available within 4,000 ft [S, OCR-garbled 1901 source; treat as approx]. A 1901 retrospective says the Commission's main error was assuming coal exports would stay at twelve million tons [S]. *Lever design*: a "Commission" that buys time and Mandate but whose forecast is falsified by exactly the rebound it ignored.
- **Clean Air Act 1956**: smokeless zones and grants to convert grates. It worked on smog, and fuel switching to gas and electricity did more than burning less. *Lever design*: substitution that cures the local pressure while moving the carbon elsewhere.

### 1.7 Ironies a dry writer will enjoy

- **Watt was paid by the coal he saved.** Boulton & Watt licensed engines for "a third part in value of any saving in fuel" against an equivalent Newcomen engine, up to 1800 [S]. Cornish mine owners came to resent paying for coal they hadn't burned, and there were years of lawsuits [S]. The first efficiency business model, and its revenue was literally the rebound gap.
- **Savery's engine burned no coal because it burned too much** (Jevons).
- **Newcomen had to work under Savery's patent**, which Parliament extended to 1733: the better machine paid rent to the worse one.
- **Duty was a league table.** Cornish engineers competed monthly in published duty reports: gamified efficiency in 1811. Efficiency was a sport, and the score was published; total coal burned was not.
- **The Royal Commission found coal abundant in 1871**; British output then nearly tripled to its 1913 peak.
- **Britain closed its last deep mine (2015) and its last coal power station (2024)** within a decade in which world coal demand set an all-time record [V].

## 2. Iron and steel (candidate sector; currently absent)

### 2.1 Efficiency path

| Step | Date | Who | Figure |
|---|---|---|---|

### 2.2 Rebound evidence

Jevons, Scotland 1830–1863 [V]: coal per ton of iron fell to under a third, and total coal consumed in Scottish ironmaking rose tenfold. Annual Scottish and UK pig-iron series were not found this pass. Mitchell's *British Historical Statistics* is the place to get them; do not invent tonnages.

### 2.3 Applications

Railways (rails, then steel rails), iron ships (then steel hulls), bridges, girders and the skyscraper frame, machine tools, tinplate and canned food, barbed wire and wire rope, the steel-framed everything. Cheap iron is the classic **indirect rebound**: it lowers the price of the capital goods of every other coal-burning industry.

### 2.4 Oddities

- A process sold as making steel "without fuel" fed one of the most coal-hungry industries on earth.
- Hot blast royalties made Neilson rich on fuel saved, which is Watt's business model again.

## 3. Lighting (Era 2 core)

### 3.1 Efficiency path

Efficacy figures from Nordhaus's reconstruction as reported second-hand [S]: open fire 0.00235 lm/W · sesame-oil lamp 0.0597 · spermaceti candle 0.1009 · whale-oil lamp 0.1346 · early town-gas lamp 0.2464 · Edison carbon-filament lamp (1883) 2.6 lm/W. Later figures are [approx]: tungsten filament ~10–15 lm/W; fluorescent tube ~50–100; LED ~100–200 by the 2020s.

### 3.2 Total consumption: the rebound evidence

Fouquet & Pearson (2006), abstract [V, LSE repository]: by 2000, UK GDP per capita was 15× its 1800 level; lighting services cost **less than 1/3,000** of their 1800 price; **per-capita lighting use was 6,500× greater**; **total lighting consumption was 25,000× greater**. A secondary summary also gives a 37,000-fold per-capita rise from 1700 to 2000 [S]. Our World in Data puts the price fall since the 1300s at about 16,000-fold: about £34,000 per million lumen-hours then against £2.15 in 2023 (2000 prices) [S].

Tsao, Saunders et al. (2010), *J. Phys. D*, "Solid-state lighting: an energy-economics perspective": world energy for lighting shows roughly **100% rebound** across 300 years, six continents and five technologies [S, authors' later abstract]. The often-quoted constant "~0.72% of GDP spent on light" is from this paper [unverified this pass]. The authors later complained (Saunders & Tsao 2012, "Rebound effects for lighting", *Energy Policy*) that *The Economist* and the *New York Times* misread them as saying efficient lighting was pointless [S]. Good broadsheet material: a paper about rebound, misreported, then rebutted.

**After LEDs: light pollution.** Kyba et al. 2023 (*Science*) used more than 50,000 citizen-science star counts (Globe at Night, 2011–2022) and found sky brightness rising by about **9.6% a year** (range 7–10%; North America 10.4%, Europe ~6.5%) [V, NOIRLab/press]. Satellites had measured a ~2.2% a year rise in the lit area/radiance for 2012–2016 (Kyba et al. 2017, *Science Advances*) [S]. Satellites under-read blue-rich LED light, which is why ground observers see more [S].

### 3.3 Applications

Street lighting (gas, Pall Mall 1807); night shifts in mills; theatres (the Savoy, 1881); shop windows and illuminated signs; the evening as leisure (reading, the music hall); domestic electric light; floodlit sport; neon advertising; 24-hour retail; architectural façade lighting; LED billboards and screens; decorative LED strips, "festoon" garden lights and the year-round fairy light. Each is a use that was unaffordable at the previous price.

### 3.4 Pressures

Coal smoke from gasworks; gasworks pollution and explosions; whale-stock depletion (a renewable stock driven down by demand, then rescued by substitution); skyglow and ecological light pollution; the lost Milky Way.

### 3.5 Restraint and levers

- **Phoebus cartel**: a lever that shortened product life to sell more lamps. An anti-efficiency cartel; it backfired on reputation, not on energy.
- **Lamp bans** (EU, UK): banning the inefficient lamp is a standard. It worked for lamp energy; skyglow rose anyway.
- **Dark-sky ordinances** (e.g. Flagstaff, Arizona, an early adopter [approx]): a cap on the *service*, not on the fuel. This is the "Sufficiency" lever in pure form.
- **Wartime blackout** (1939–45): the only time in this record that lighting use fell to near zero by decree.

### 3.6 Ironies

- **Kerosene "saved the whales"** is the standard story; the record is more mixed (whale-oil demand was already falling) [S]. Use it as a thing people say.
- **"We will make electricity so cheap that only the rich will burn candles"** is attributed to Edison [unverified; probably apocryphal]. It is also, in 2026, true: candles are now a luxury good.
- **Light became almost free and we spend about the same share of income on it**: the Tsao constancy, if the 0.72% figure is confirmed.
- **The incumbent fights back with efficiency**: the gas mantle extended gas's life against the bulb, exactly as the Cornish engine extended steam's.

## 4. Heating and cooling (candidate sector)

### 4.1 Heating

- **Count Rumford** (Benjamin Thompson), essay *Of Chimney Fire-places* (1796) [approx]: the shallow, angled-cheek "Rumford fireplace" sent far more heat into the room. He also designed a drip coffee-pot [approx]. A scientist-adventurer who suits a cameo.
- **UK home temperatures ("temperature takeback")**: the government's *Housing Energy Fact File* says most homes in 1970 averaged about **12 °C** in winter [S], with a rise of about **4 °C** since [S]. Press coverage of the 2014 edition gave 13.7 °C (1970) and **17.7 °C** (2014 average) [S]. **Caveat**: Vadodaria, Loveday & Haines (2014, *Energy Policy*) found little or no rise in *living-room* temperatures when occupied, 1969–2010 [S]. The rise is mostly in *more rooms heated for longer* (central heating). This is comfort-taking: the rebound arrives as warmth and space, not as more heat per room.
- **Direct rebound for space heating**: best guess **10–30%** (range 1–60%, 9 studies, medium confidence), from Sorrell 2007 via secondary slides [S]. Another UK study gives **70%** for heating [S].
- **Insulation paradox**: rebound is highest in cold, fuel-poor homes, where the saving is taken as health and comfort. Whether that counts as a failure is the moral of the sector: **a rebound that is welfare** (see the Candidates section).

### 4.2 Cooling and the cold chain

- **Ice trade**: Frederic Tudor shipped New England ice to the Caribbean from 1806 [approx].
- **Air conditioning**: Willis Carrier, humidity control for the Sackett-Wilhelms printing works, Brooklyn, 1902 [approx].
- **Refrigerants**: CFCs (Thomas Midgley, c. 1928–30) [approx] replaced toxic ammonia and sulphur dioxide; then they destroyed stratospheric ozone. Montreal Protocol 1987; HFC replacements were potent greenhouse gases, so the Kigali Amendment (2016) [approx] followed. **A substitution lever that fixed one pressure and created the next, twice.**
- **Refrigerators (US)**: a new refrigerator uses about **one-quarter** of the energy of a 1973 model while offering about **20% more storage** at about **half the price** [S, US DOE fact sheet]; another DOE document says one-third [S]. California's standards (1976/1978, sources differ) led to the federal ones [S]. A rare clean efficiency success: the service (cold litres) saturated. The record is the second fridge in the garage [unverified as a statistic, true as an observation].
- **Global cooling** (IEA, *The Future of Cooling*, 2018): air conditioners and fans use about **10% of global electricity**; the AC stock was ~1.6 billion units, heading for **5.6 billion by 2050** ("10 new ACs sold every second for the next 30 years"); cooling energy demand is projected to **more than triple** by 2050 without efficiency policy, and efficiency could cut it 45% against the reference case [S/V].
- **Feedback loop**: cooling emits carbon, which warms the climate, which raises cooling demand. The one service in this domain whose pressure feeds its own demand.

## 5. Motion: rail, sea, road, air, containers (Era 3 and candidates)

### 5.1 Roads and cars

- **Induced traffic**:
  - **Downs (1962)**, "The Law of Peak-Hour Expressway Congestion" (*Traffic Quarterly*): new expressway capacity fills up at peak [S].
  - **Thomson (1977)**, *Great Cities and their Traffic*: road speeds tend toward the speed of public transport, which gives the **Downs–Thomson paradox**: widening roads can make everyone slower if transit loses riders and service [approx].
  - **Mogridge (1990)**, *Travel in Towns: Jam Yesterday, Jam Today and Jam Tomorrow?*: the Lewis–Mogridge position, that traffic expands to fill the road space [approx; not verified this pass].
  - **SACTRA (1994)**, *Trunk Roads and the Generation of Traffic*: the UK government's advisory committee accepted induced traffic [approx].
  - **Duranton & Turner (2011, *AER*)**, "The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion": vehicle-km travelled rises in proportion to interstate lane-km (elasticity ~1.03) [V]. The extra traffic comes from residents driving more, commercial traffic and in-migration. Public transport showed no effect on VKT [V]. Turner's own caveat: "We can only claim that this is a rule within the range of data we can observe" [S].
- **Braess's paradox (1968)**: adding a link can slow a network [S]. Real-world cases: the temporary closure of New York's 42nd Street on Earth Day 1990, when traffic reportedly improved [S, NYT via blogs]; the demolition of Seoul's Cheonggyecheon elevated road (2003–05) [S]. **The only lever in the domain where deleting capacity improves the service**: a direct analogue of Era 5's "Deletion".
- **Car fuel economy (US)**:
  - The Energy Policy and Conservation Act (Dec 1975) created **CAFE** [approx].
  - EPA trends: fuel economy rose fast from 1975 to the early 1980s while weight and horsepower fell. For about two decades before 2004, power and weight rose while fuel economy *fell* [V, EPA].
  - Light trucks reached 48% of production in MY2004 [S]; truck SUVs alone are now about **half of all new US vehicles** (EPA Trends 2025) [V]. Cars and wagons fell to 26% in MY2021, from 50% as recently as 2013 [S].
  - **The SUV loophole**: light trucks had a laxer CAFE standard, and the 1978 Gas Guzzler Tax applies to cars only [approx]. The efficiency rule pushed the market to the vehicle class with the weaker rule.
- **Direct rebound for car travel**: best guess **10–30%** (range 3–87%, 17 studies, high confidence) in Sorrell 2007 via secondary slides [S]; another UK study says 54% [S].

### 5.2 Oil shocks and restraint

- **October 1973** OAPEC embargo. UK:
  - a 50 mph limit on all roads including motorways, announced 5 Dec 1973 and in force from about 8 Dec [V, Hansard];
  - **petrol ration coupons printed and partly distributed but never used** [S];
  - the three-day week, Jan–Mar 1974 (driven as much by the miners' dispute) [approx].
- **US**:
  - a national 55 mph limit (1974), repealed 1995 [approx];
  - Carter's televised fireside chat in a cardigan (2 Feb 1977) [approx];
  - "Don't be fuelish" posters [approx].
- **UK fuel duty escalator** (lever that worked, then backfired politically):
  - introduced by Norman Lamont in the March 1993 Budget at 3% a year above inflation, explicitly to cut road-transport emissions [S];
  - raised to 5%, then 6% under Labour (3 points above inflation 1993–97, 6 points 1997–99) [S];
  - abandoned in the 1999 Pre-Budget Report (IFS) or in 2000 (other sources) [S, disputed];
  - followed by the September 2000 fuel protests: refinery blockades from 8 Sep, ~3,000 petrol stations closed by 12 Sep [S].
  - *Lever design*: a "Fuel duty escalator" that works but accumulates **Resentment**; past a threshold a crisis event wipes it out and costs Mandate.

### 5.3 Rail and sea

See 1.3 for the steam dates and Suez. **Containerisation**: Malcom McLean's *Ideal-X* sailed from Port Newark on 26 April 1956 with 58 containers [S]. McLean's own reckoning put loading at **$5.83/ton conventionally against 15.8 cents** for the box [S, Levinson via NYRB]. The reckoning leaves out the cost of the boxes and cranes [S]. Transport cost collapses, so supply chains globalise, so production moves to where energy is cheapest and dirtiest. That is **carbon leakage**: the importer's statistics improve while the world's get worse.

### 5.4 Aviation

- New-aircraft fuel burn fell about **45% from 1968 to 2014** (1.3% a year) [V, ICCT]. On seat-km and tonne-km bases new-aircraft efficiency roughly doubled from 1960 to 2008, but improved substantially in only two of the five decades [S, ICCT 2009].
- 2010–2019: fleet fuel efficiency per RPK improved **>2.5% a year** while RPK grew **>6% a year** [S, IEA]. 90 g CO₂ per RPK in 2019 [S, ICCT]. Aviation emissions **roughly doubled since 1990** [S, OWID].
- Efficiency comes from engines, but also from denser seating and higher load factors: the low-cost model turns efficiency directly into cheaper fares. Total world RPK in 2019 (~8.7 trillion) [unverified].

## 6. Electricity, the grid and appliances (Era 2/4 bridge; candidate sector)

- **Pearl Street**, New York, 4 Sep 1882 (Edison) [approx].
- **Samuel Insull** (Chicago Edison from 1892) [S]:
  - discovered that fixed capital, not fuel, was the cost to beat, so he **built load**;
  - offered cheap off-peak power to streetcars, elevated railways, ice houses and factories to raise his load factor [S];
  - had a two-tier rate by 1897 [S];
  - used the **Wright demand rate** (a declining block whose high-priced first block is sized by the customer's peak demand), named for Arthur Wright of Brighton; Insull held a financial interest in the Wright patents [S, *J. Econ. Hist.*]. That Insull first saw the meter on a visit to Brighton is often told [unverified].
  - Real US prices fell to about $0.38/kWh by 1909 [S, IAEE; units as given].
  - His holding-company empire collapsed in 1932 [approx].
  - **The supplier as the engine of rebound**: lower prices raise sales, which raise the load factor, which lowers the cost per kWh, which lowers prices again.
- **Electrical marketing**:
  - "Reddy Kilowatt", the utility mascot from 1926 [approx];
  - "Live Better Electrically", the 1950s US industry campaign with "Medallion" all-electric homes [approx].
  - Appliance waves: irons, fans, washing machines, fridges, TV, AC, the second TV, standby power, chargers, servers.
- **UK**: the Electricity (Supply) Act 1926 created the Central Electricity Board and the national grid (substantially complete by the early 1930s) [approx]. UK electricity demand reached a record low of **316.8 TWh in 2023** [V, DUKES 2024], 319 TWh in 2024, 323 TWh in 2025 [V, DUKES 2026]. It is below its 2000s level (a peak around 2005 is commonly cited [unverified]). Efficient appliances, the loss of heavy industry (leakage) and higher prices all contributed. **An absolute decline in a rich country, partly bought by offshoring.**
- **The reverse lever: revenue decoupling.** Breaking the link between utility profit and kWh sold (California from the early 1980s [approx]) is the institutional fix to Insull's incentive. It is a natural "Institution" in the game.

## 7. The economists and the debate

| Who | Year | Work | Claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| W. S. Jevons | 1865/66 | *The Coal Question* | Efficiency raises total coal use: "the very contrary is the truth" [V]. |
| Daniel Khazzoom | 1980 | "Economic Implications of Mandated Efficiency Standards for Household Appliances", *Energy Journal* 1(4) [S] | California's appliance standards ignored rebound. |
| Leonard Brookes | 1979 onwards; 1990 *Energy Policy* [approx] | UKAEA chief economist | Economy-wide efficiency raises energy demand [S]. |
| Harry Saunders | 1992 | "The Khazzoom–Brookes Postulate and Neoclassical Growth", *Energy Journal* 13(4) [S] | Named the postulate: "with fixed real energy prices, energy efficiency gains will increase energy consumption above what it would be without these gains" [S]. Two channels: energy looks cheaper relative to other inputs, and growth [S]. |
| Greening, Greene & Difiglio | 2000 | "Energy efficiency and consumption — the rebound effect — a survey", *Energy Policy* 28 [approx] | The standard taxonomy; direct rebounds mostly modest. |
| Steve Sorrell (UKERC) | 2007 | *The Rebound Effect: an assessment of the evidence for economy-wide energy savings from improved energy efficiency* [V, title] | Rebound "merits explicit treatment" in policy [S]. Household direct rebound generally under 30% in the OECD [S]. Economy-wide rebound is larger and poorly measured. |
| Gillingham, Kotchen, Rapson & Wagner | 2013 | "The rebound effect is overplayed", *Nature* 493: 475–476 [V] | Rebound is real but "has become a distraction"; it is "no excuse for inaction" [S]. Later: Gillingham, Rapson & Wagner, "The Rebound Effect and Energy Efficiency Policy" (*REEP*, 2016) [approx]. |
| Brockway, Sorrell, Semieniuk, Heun & Court | 2021 | "Energy efficiency and economy-wide rebound effects: A review of the evidence and its implications", *RSER* 141: 110781 [V] | Economy-wide rebound may offset **more than half** of expected savings; scenario models underweight it [V]. A companion blog gives a mean of 71% across 12 studies [S]. |
| Tsao, Saunders et al. | 2010; 2012 | Lighting papers (see 3.2) | ~100% rebound in lighting over 300 years [S]. |

**Sector rule of thumb for the balancer** (by the literature's best guesses, not law):

- household direct rebound: ~10–30%;
- producer and industrial: higher and poorly measured;
- economy-wide: likely >50% (Brockway 2021);
- backfire: documented for general-purpose technologies at an early stage of adoption (steam, light), which is the game's core claim; disputed for mature consumer services.

**The sceptics' best lines**: rebound is "overplayed" (Gillingham et al.); fridges show efficiency *can* save, because the service saturates; the Tsao authors themselves argue for promoting better lighting. The game's design (elastic early, saturated late) is the reconciliation, and the sceptics belong in it as recurring voices, not as straw men.

## 8. Candidates for new eras/sectors within this domain

Ranked by how *distinct a rebound mechanism* each offers, not by how fresh the skin is.

1. **Electricity utilities: "The Load-Builder" (Insull).** *Mechanism*: **supply-side rebound**. The player is the seller with fixed capital, so the cheapest kWh is the next one sold. Declining block tariffs and off-peak deals deliberately build demand. Efficiency lowers cost per unit only if load grows; the player is paid to rebound. *Counter-lever*: **revenue decoupling** (an Institution) flips the incentive. No existing era has an actor whose profit depends on manufacturing demand.
2. **Roads: "Induced Traffic".** *Mechanism*: **capacity is the efficiency, time is the price**. A road upgrade lowers the time cost, not the fuel cost, and demand fills it back to the old congestion level (Duranton & Turner's elasticity ≈1). It adds Downs–Thomson coupling to a transit sub-service, so improving roads can *worsen* the service. Braess makes **removing capacity** a lever that improves outcomes, mirroring Era 5's Deletion. Equilibrium restoration is a new shape: the service price springs back.
3. **Cooling: "The Heat Dome Loop".** *Mechanism*: **pressure-driven demand**. Carbon raises temperature, temperature raises cooling saturation, cooling raises carbon. The only service whose pressure feeds its own demand curve (positive feedback). It also gives a **substitution lever that backfires serially** (ammonia → CFC → HFC: each fix spawns the next pressure, and Montreal and Kigali are the levers that worked).
4. **Iron and steel: "Cheap Capital".** *Mechanism*: **efficiency in an intermediate good lowers the price of capital for every other era** (Jevons's indirect effect, "cheap iron ... accelerating other coal-consuming branches"). Mechanically, steel efficiency multiplies the *capital cost* term `c` across all eras rather than adding demand to one. Reserve expansion (Gilchrist–Thomas lets phosphoric ore count) and "without fuel" headlines come free.
5. **Heating: "Comfort Taking".** *Mechanism*: **rebound as welfare with a ceiling**. Efficiency gains are taken as warmth (12 °C → ~17.7 °C, more rooms heated) up to a comfort saturation, and the taken-back energy *raises wellbeing* (hence Hindsight), not income. It forces the player to ask whether a rebound is bad. Distinct because the rebound pays out in the scoring currency, not in Capital.
6. **Containers and shipping: "Leakage".** *Mechanism*: **spatial rebound / carbon leakage**. Cheap transport lets production move to where energy is dirtiest. The player's *home* resource gauge falls while a hidden world gauge rises (Britain's coal exit against the record 2024 world demand). It suggests a "Consumption-based accounting" Institution that reveals the hidden gauge, the way the Statistics Office reveals the rebound line.
7. **Aviation: "Load Factor".** *Mechanism*: efficiency via **utilisation** (denser seats, fuller planes, low-cost model) passes straight to fares with near-zero capital lag. Closer to a re-skin of Motion; its distinct hook is that the efficiency *is* the price cut, so k is very high.
8. **Lighting extension: "Skyglow".** Already Era 2; a post-LED coda where the service becomes essentially free and the pressure is **non-fuel** (lost dark sky, ~9.6%/yr). Useful as a late-game "sufficiency only" puzzle, but mechanically close to the existing era.
