# Rebound beyond coal and silicon: sector source book

Source material for new eras and sectors in *Rebound*. Each section is laid out for mining:
**Efficiency steps** (upgrade trees) · **Total use** (the gauge that refuses to fall) · **Applications** (uses opened by
each price fall) · **Pressures and collapses** · **Restraint tried** (cross-referenced to `restraint.md`) · **Characters,
quotations, headlines** · **Ironies**.

Conventions:

- **[verified]** is implied for anything with a source and no tag.
- **[approx.]** means sources agree on the order of magnitude but differ on the figure; the range is given where known.
- **[disputed]** means the scholarly literature disagrees, and both sides are given.
- **[unverified]** means I could not confirm it against a source during this pass. Do not use these in game text without a check.
- Quotations are given only where the wording was confirmed, or are flagged.

Section numbers refer to the source list at the end ([S1], [S2]...).

## 1. Agriculture and land

The core question for a farming era: does higher yield per hectare spare land (the "Borlaug hypothesis") or does
cheaper food pull more land into the plough (Jevons applied to land)? The honest answer from the literature is
*both, depending on whether the market is local or global* — which is itself a mechanic.

### 1.1 Efficiency steps (an upgrade tree)

| Date | Step | What it did | Notes for writers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1604 → c.1750–1830 peak | **Parliamentary enclosure** | Over 5,200 enclosure bills covering about 6.8 million acres between 1604 and 1914, roughly a fifth of England. Chapman's estimate is higher: c.7.25–7.35 million acres by parliamentary means [S1]. First act: Radipole, Dorset, 1604. | Enclosure is an efficiency upgrade that is also a commons-abolition. Good card: "+yield, −commoners". |
| mid-18th century | **Norfolk four-course rotation** (wheat, turnips, barley, clover) | Removed the fallow year; clover fixes nitrogen, turnips feed stock over winter, more stock means more manure. Overton argues enclosure made the rotation adoptable; it was not widely known until the mid-18th century [S1]. | Charles, 2nd Viscount Townshend ("Turnip Townshend") is the popular hero; his real role is promoted rather than proven. Use the nickname, not a claim of invention. |
| 1840–1880 | **Guano** (Peruvian seabird droppings) | Peru exported about 12.7 million tons of guano 1840–1880, sale value of about £150 million [S2]. Chincha Islands deposits nearly exhausted by the late 1860s. | A stock resource with a sharp depletion curve inside a single generation. |
| 1856 | US **Guano Islands Act** | Let a US citizen claim any unclaimed island holding guano; around 100 islands were claimed in the Pacific and Caribbean [S2]. | Statute that annexes territory for fertiliser. Ideal headline material. |
| 1879–1884 | **War of the Pacific** | Chile took Peru's Tarapacá and Bolivia's coast, with their guano and nitrate (saltpetre) fields; Treaty of Ancón 1884 [S2]. Sometimes called the "Guano War" or "Saltpetre War". | A resource war over fertiliser. The Atacama nitrate boom followed until synthetic ammonia killed it. |
| 1898 | **Crookes's warning** | Sir William Crookes, presidential address to the British Association (1898), warned of a coming wheat/nitrogen shortage and called on chemists to fix atmospheric nitrogen. [Wording of the famous "deadly peril" line unverified; date and gist are standard.] | The Victorian peak-fertiliser scare. |
| 1909 (lab), 1913 (Oppau plant) | **Haber–Bosch ammonia synthesis** | By 2000, about 44% of humanity was sustained by Haber–Bosch nitrogen; about 48% by 2008 (Erisman et al. 2008). Smil's earlier estimate was c.40% by 2000 [S3]. Model-based approximations. | Single biggest efficiency step in the history of food. |
| 1940s–1960s | **Green Revolution**: semi-dwarf wheat (Borlaug, Mexico from 1944), IR8 rice (IRRI, 1966), plus irrigation and fertiliser | Stevenson et al. (2013) estimate Green Revolution crop germplasm research saved 18–27 million hectares from conversion 1965–2004 [S4]. Hertel et al. (2014) estimate a far larger figure (cited at 144 Mha by Breakthrough) [S5]. | Borlaug: Nobel Peace Prize 1970. |
| 1961–2009 | **Aggregate yield gain** | Ausubel, Wernick & Waggoner ("Peak Farmland", 2013): land farmed grew only 12% while the crop production index rose c.300%; producing the 2009 harvest at 1961 yields would have needed roughly three times the land (2009 needed c.35% of the land 1961 methods would have) [S6]. | The optimists' best number. |

### 1.2 Total use: does cheaper food spare land?

- **The Borlaug hypothesis**: raising yields spares natural land. Stevenson et al. (PNAS 2013) "generally support the
  Borlaug hypothesis" for 1965–2004 [S4].
- **The Jevons counter-case**: Rudel et al. (PNAS 2009, "Agricultural intensification and changes in cultivated areas,
  1970–2005") looked for land sparing at the country level and concluded that cropland abandonment "cannot be assumed
  without explicit policy intervention" [S7]. In most countries intensification and expansion went together.
- **The synthesis — the sign depends on trade**: Hertel, Ramankutty & Baldos (PNAS 2014) found the historic Green
  Revolution in Asia, Latin America and the Middle East was "unambiguously land and emissions sparing" globally, but
  that a future African Green Revolution, in an integrated world market, "could increase crop land use and CO2
  emissions" [S5]. Mechanism: if a region's productivity rises and it can export, its farms become more competitive,
  so its *own* land use rises even while the world's falls. Both models found higher land use *within* the improving
  region and net sparing globally [S5].
- **Game mechanic proposal**: an efficiency card whose rebound sign flips with a "Trade" slider. Closed economy:
  yield up → price falls → demand is inelastic (people eat only so much) → land is spared. Open economy: yield up →
  your exports win market share → your hectares grow. **Jevons by comparative advantage.**

### 1.3 Meat: indirect use (the calories that go through animals)

- Cassidy, West, Gerber & Foley (Environmental Research Letters 2013): **36% of crop calories go to animal feed; only
  12% of those feed calories end up in human diets** as meat, milk and eggs. Growing food only for direct human
  consumption could, in principle, raise available food calories by up to 70%, enough for about 4 billion more people
  [S8]. Of all calories grown in the 41 crops studied, 59% reach the food system.
- This is the cleanest real example of a **conversion loss that grows with wealth**: richer diets route more calories
  through animals. Every yield gain makes feed cheaper, which makes meat cheaper, which raises meat demand.
- Livestock and antibiotics are linked: see §7.4.

### 1.4 Palm oil: the efficient crop that cleared the forest

- World palm oil production rose about **40-fold, from c.2 million tonnes in 1970 to c.80 million tonnes in 2020**
  (Our World in Data) [S9]. In 2018, 84% came from Indonesia and Malaysia. Planted area rose from about 4 million
  hectares (1980) to 19 million hectares (2018) [S9].
- **The efficiency paradox**: palm yields far more oil per hectare than soy, rapeseed or sunflower — OWID says "up to 20
  times"; CIRAD-based work says 5–8 times [approx.]. OWID revised its palm yield down from 4 to 2.8 t/ha after FAO
  updates [S9]. A Nature Plants study: meeting demand growth with palm would need c.36 Mha more land; with soy c.204 Mha
  [S9].
- **Deforestation share** [disputed]: oil palm was the largest single driver of deforestation in Indonesia 2001–2016 at
  about 23% (OWID); in Malaysian Borneo new plantations accounted for about 50% of deforestation 1972–2015; in
  peninsular Malaysia today the share is below 1% [S9].
- So: the most land-efficient oil became the cheapest oil, which made it the default fat in food, cosmetics and
  biodiesel, which made it the most planted. A boycott that switches to a less efficient oil would need *more* land.
  **The efficient choice and the expanding choice are the same crop.**

### 1.5 Pressures and collapses

- Peru's guano: exhausted within c.40 years (§1.1).
- Nitrogen run-off and dead zones (Gulf of Mexico, Baltic) — standard, not checked in this pass for figures.
- Soil: the US Dust Bowl (1930s) followed mechanised ploughing of the southern plains [figures unverified in this pass].

### 1.6 Characters, quotations, headlines

- **Norman Borlaug**, Nobel Peace Prize 1970. His Nobel lecture is often quoted as saying the Green Revolution had won
  "a temporary success in man's war against hunger" and given "a breathing space" [wording to verify against the Nobel
  lecture text before use].
- **Fritz Haber**: Nobel Prize in Chemistry for 1918 (awarded 1919) for ammonia synthesis; also directed the German
  chlorine gas attack at Ypres in 1915. Same chemistry (fixed nitrogen) made fertiliser and explosives.
- **William Crookes** (1898), the alarmist who was right about the problem and right about the fix.
- **Turnip Townshend** (popular nickname; treat his priority as legend).

### 1.7 Ironies

- The Guano Islands Act (1856) is still on the US statute book [unverified that it is unrepealed; widely reported].
- Haber–Bosch both fed half the world and made the explosives of two world wars.
- The highest-yield oil crop is the one most associated with deforestation.
- "Peak farmland" (2013) was declared by the optimists; the land-sparing evidence is strongest exactly where markets
  are least connected.

## 2. Water and irrigation

The best rebound mechanism outside energy, because it adds an **accounting illusion**: efficiency cuts the water a
farmer *applies*, but what matters to the basin is the water *consumed* (evaporated, transpired, embedded in crops).
"Wasted" water from flood irrigation mostly returned to rivers and aquifers. Drip irrigation stops that return flow,
lets the farmer grow more or thirstier crops, and the basin loses more.

### 2.1 Efficiency steps

| Step | Field application efficiency (rule of thumb) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Flood / furrow | low | Most "loss" is return flow to aquifer or river. |
| Centre pivot (Frank Zybach patent, 1952) [patent date unverified] | medium | Made the High Plains irrigable from the Ogallala. |
| Low-energy precision / dropped nozzles | higher | Subsidised in western Kansas (see Pfeiffer & Lin below). |
| Drip / micro-irrigation | highest | Subsidised in India, Morocco, Spain, US. |

(Efficiency percentages vary by source and crop; the game should use relative steps, not quoted numbers.)

### 2.2 Total use: the irrigation efficiency paradox

- **Grafton et al., "The paradox of irrigation efficiency", *Science* 361 (2018)**, DOI 10.1126/science.aat9314:
  advanced irrigation technologies "frequently increase on-farm water consumption and groundwater extractions while
  also reducing return flows" [S10]. Mechanisms: more water used per crop, switch to thirstier crops, expansion of
  irrigated area. Cases: **Rajasthan**, where drip subsidies increased the volume of water applied and consumed
  locally; **Morocco's Souss and Tensift basins**, where drip adoption reduced recharge to over-exploited aquifers
  [S10]. Irrigation is c.70% of global water extractions and c.40% of the world's food calories (IFPRI) [S10].
- **Pfeiffer & Lin, *JEEM* 67(2) (2014)**, "Does efficient irrigation technology lead to reduced groundwater
  extraction?": in western Kansas, state and federal cost-share programmes paid farmers to switch from standard
  centre pivots to dropped-nozzle systems. **Groundwater extraction rose**, mainly because farmers shifted to more
  water-intensive crops [S11]. A cleanly measured direct rebound in a subsidy programme meant to save water.
- **Ward & Pulido-Velazquez, *PNAS* 105(47) (2008)**, "Water conservation in irrigation can increase water use":
  basin model of the Upper Rio Grande. "Water conservation subsidies are unlikely to reduce water use under conditions
  that occur in many river basins"; efficient technology "reduces valuable return flows and limits aquifer recharge";
  real savings need accounting that tracks *depletion* [S12].

### 2.3 Pressures and collapses

**The Aral Sea** (Kazakhstan/Uzbekistan)
- Soviet planners diverted the Amu Darya and Syr Darya, from the 1960s, to irrigate cotton.
- Losses since 1960 [approx.]: about 88% of surface area and 92% of volume by recent counts; other accounts give c.70%
  of area and 90% of volume by 2002, or "90 percent" loosely [S13]. The sea split into a North (Kazakh) and South
  (Uzbek) sea by about 1990.
- **Kok-Aral dam** (completed 2005; c.US$86 million, World Bank-backed): the North Aral has partly recovered — area
  from 2,850 km² (2003) to 3,250 km² (2006); later claims of a 12 m level rise and a 68% volume increase by 2008 come
  from weaker sources [S13]. The South Aral has not recovered.
- An attributed Soviet line that the Aral was a "mistake of nature" or should "die beautifully" circulates widely
  [unverified; do not quote].

**The Ogallala / High Plains aquifer** (USGS)
- Area-weighted average water-level decline of **16.5 feet from predevelopment (c.1950) to 2019**; recoverable storage
  down **286.4 million acre-feet** over the same period [S14]. In 2015 storage was c.2.91 billion acre-feet, down c.9%
  from predevelopment [S14].
- Declines are concentrated: the 2013 report gives local declines of 158 ft (Texas) and 67 ft (Kansas) 1950–2013 [S14;
  read as water-level declines].
- Decline slowed sharply after 2013, then continued [S14].

**Saudi Arabia's fossil-water wheat** [approx., figures to verify]: from the late 1970s the kingdom subsidised wheat
on non-renewable aquifers, became an exporter, then phased domestic wheat purchase out by 2016. A self-sufficiency
policy that mined a non-renewable stock and then ended. Good collapse arc; check numbers before use.

**The Colorado River Compact (1922)** [approx.]: apportioned the river on wet-year flow estimates (c.16.5–17.5 million
acre-feet a year) when the long-term flow is markedly lower; Lake Mead hit record lows in 2022. A quota fixed on an
optimistic baseline is permanently over-allocated. Verify exact figures before use.

### 2.4 Ironies

- The "waste" in flood irrigation was the basin's recharge.
- Subsidies for water saving raised water use in Kansas [S11].
- The North Aral was saved by a dam — the same technology that killed the whole sea.

## 3. Fishing

The strongest candidate for a new mechanism: a **renewable stock that collapses past a threshold and does not come
back**, a **gauge that lies** (catch per unit effort stays high while the stock falls, because the fleet keeps finding
the last shoals), and **rivals** (foreign fleets on the same banks).

### 3.1 Efficiency steps

| Date | Step | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1880s | **Steam trawlers** (England) | Industrial bottom trawling begins; the Thurstan data series starts in 1889 [S15]. |
| 1883 | (Rhetoric, not tech) **Thomas Huxley's inexhaustibility address** | See quotations. |
| 1920s–30s | **Echo sounders** | Depth then fish finding. [Dates approximate.] |
| 1954 | **Fairtry**, the first purpose-built factory freezer stern trawler (Salvesen, Leith) [unverified in this pass; standard in the histories] | Fleet could stay out for months and process at sea. Soviet "Pushkin" class copied the design [unverified]. |
| 1950s–70s | **Factory freezer trawlers, fish-finding sonar, larger nets, radar, later GPS** | Grand Banks factory trawlers described as "floating fish plants" that could stay out for months [S16]. |

### 3.2 Total use: catch vs stock (the Grand Banks)

- Grand Banks groundfish catches more than doubled from **360,000 tons (1959) to 810,000 tons (1968)** — or c.800,000
  tonnes for northern cod alone, depending on source [S16]. **Foreign fleets took about 80% of the 1968 catch** [S16].
- Harvestable cod biomass fell **82% between 1962 and 1977** (later estimates) [S16].
- **Canada's 200-mile limit, 1 January 1977**, partly to push foreign trawlers out [S16]. One review: before 1977
  foreign trawlers were the overfishers; after 1977 "this part was played mainly by Canadian trawlers" [S16]. The
  nationalised commons was fished down by the nation.
- Hutchings & Myers (1995): as much northern cod was caught in fifteen years (1960–1975) as in the preceding period
  they compared it with [S16; wording of the comparison period is loose in the secondary source].
- By 1992 the spawning biomass of northern cod was about **1% of its estimated peak**; a separate measure gives a c.93%
  fall 1962–1992 [S17]. Different baselines, both usable with attribution.

### 3.3 Collapse and its aftermath

- **2 July 1992**: Fisheries Minister **John Crosbie** announced a two-year moratorium on northern cod [S17].
- Roughly **30,000 jobs** lost in Newfoundland and Labrador (the most cited figure); the Canadian Encyclopedia gives
  some 40,000 fishers and related workers affected; often called the largest industrial closure in Canadian history
  [S17].
- The "two-year" moratorium lasted **32 years**; a commercial northern cod fishery was reopened in **2024** [S17].
- **Hysteresis**: the stock did not recover for decades after fishing stopped. This is the mechanic Rebound lacks: a
  Pressure that, once over its line, *changes the regeneration rate itself*.

### 3.4 The long baseline: 118 years of trawling

- **Thurstan, Brockington & Roberts, *Nature Communications* (2010)**, DOI 10.1038/ncomms1013: bottom-trawl landings
  into England and Wales 1889–2007, corrected for fishing power. **Landings per unit of fishing power fell 94% — a
  17-fold decline — over 118 years** [S15]. Press coverage: halibut LPUP down 500-fold, haddock more than 100-fold,
  plaice, wolffish, hake and ling more than 20-fold [S15].
- In other words, it takes about **17 times the engine power** to land the same fish as in 1889. Every fishing-power
  upgrade was an efficiency gain against a shrinking stock; the efficiency gauge rose, the stock gauge fell, and the
  catch stayed roughly level until it did not.
- Seafish (industry body) disputed it as "dodgy data from the past" [S15] — a usable heckler line, attributed.

### 3.5 The lying gauge: hyperstability

- Catch per unit effort (CPUE) is the classic proxy for stock size. For shoaling fish, CPUE can stay high while the
  stock collapses, because fish crowd into the remaining dense patches and modern sonar finds them. This is called
  **hyperstability**. It is a well-established concept in fisheries science; I did not retrieve a specific northern-cod
  paper on it in this pass [mark: concept verified as standard; specific cod citation unverified].
- **Shifting baselines**: Daniel Pauly coined "shifting baseline syndrome" (1995) for each generation taking the stock
  it first saw as normal [standard attribution; not re-checked here].

### 3.6 Restraint tried (see `restraint.md` §Quotas)

- 200-mile Exclusive Economic Zones (Canada 1977; UNCLOS).
- Iceland's ITQs (1984, 1990); the UN Human Rights Committee ruled against Iceland's quota allocation in 2007.
- The Cod Wars (UK–Iceland, 1958, 1972–73, 1975–76) — Iceland won all three [dates standard; not re-checked here].

### 3.7 Characters, quotations, headlines

- **Thomas Henry Huxley**, inaugural address, International Fisheries Exhibition, London, 1883, is widely quoted as
  saying that "probably all the great sea fisheries, are inexhaustible" [wording standard; check the 1883 text before
  setting it in type].
- **John Crosbie**, 1 July 1992, to angry fishermen at Bay Bulls, Newfoundland: **"I didn't take the fish from the
  goddamn water."** (Widely reported; title of a 2023 article in *The Independent* (Newfoundland) [S16].)

### 3.8 Ironies

- The 200-mile limit swapped foreign overfishing for domestic overfishing.
- The fleet's gauge of success (catch per trip) stayed healthy almost to the end.
- A "two-year" moratorium lasted 32 years.

## 4. Whaling (and forestry)

### 4.1 Whaling: efficiency steps

| Date | Step | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| 1860s–70s | **Svend Foyn's harpoon cannon** with explosive grenade, on steam catchers [standard; dates not re-checked] | Made the fast rorquals (blue, fin) catchable. |
| 1925 | **Stern slipway factory ship** (*Lancing*, commonly cited) [unverified in this pass] | Whales hauled aboard and processed on the high seas; freed whaling from shore stations and licences. |
| 1932 | **Blue Whale Unit (BWU)** quota metric [standard; not re-checked] | 1 BWU = 1 blue = 2 fin = 2.5 humpback = 6 sei. A quota denominated in oil yield, so the rational whaler took the biggest whales first. |

### 4.2 Total use

- **Rocha, Clapham & Ivashchenko (2014)**: nearly **2.9 million** large whales killed 1900–1999 (2,894,094):
  276,442 North Atlantic, 563,696 North Pacific, 2,053,956 Southern Hemisphere [S18]. Fin whales (c.874,000) and sperm
  whales (c.761,000) were the most hunted. Peak years: 1925–1939 (Southern Hemisphere) and 1946–1975 (both) [S18].
- **Antarctic blue whales**: pre-whaling c.**239,000** (95% CI 202,000–311,000); whaling 1905–1973 reduced them to a low
  of c.**360** (CI 150–840) (Branch et al.) [S19]. Recovering at c.7.3% a year (1.4–11.6%) but still below 1% of the
  original level in that analysis; a later estimate gives c.4,018 animals, 1.9% of pre-whaling [S19].

### 4.3 The substitution irony

- Kerosene (from the 1850s) displaced whale oil for lamps and is often credited with saving the sperm whale. Then
  20th-century industrial whaling — for margarine (hydrogenated whale oil), soap and glycerine for explosives — killed
  more whales than all earlier centuries [qualitative claim standard; the margarine link not re-checked in this pass].
  **A substitute saved the whales; a new application killed them.** This is the frontier rebound exactly.

### 4.4 Restraint

- IWC (1946 convention); commercial moratorium adopted 1982, effective from the 1985/86–1986 seasons [S20]; Norway and
  Japan objected; Japan's "scientific" programmes 1987–2019; ICJ ruled JARPA II not for scientific purposes (2014);
  Japan left the IWC 30 June 2019 and resumed commercial whaling in its own waters [S20]. See `restraint.md`.

### 4.5 Forestry

- **Britain's wood-to-coal transition**: coal substituted for firewood and charcoal, relieving English woodland — the
  "organic economy" to "mineral economy" shift (E. A. Wrigley) [standard; not re-checked]. Jevons's coal was itself the
  great forest-saver.
- **Forestry Commission (1919)**, created after the First World War exposed timber shortages; UK woodland cover was c.5%
  then and is c.13% now [approx.; check Forest Research statistics].
- **The forest transition** (A. S. Mather, 1992): countries deforest then reforest as they industrialise [standard;
  not re-checked]. Caveat: reforestation in rich countries often coincides with imported timber and food — land use
  exported, like consumption-based carbon.
- **The paperless office**: Sellen & Harper, *The Myth of the Paperless Office* (MIT Press, 2002), documented how office
  paper use rose after the personal computer and the laser printer made printing cheap [book standard; figures not
  re-checked]. A rebound in the IT era measured in trees.

## 5. Materials

### 5.1 Aluminium cans: lightweighting

- The first aluminium beverage cans (early 1960s) weighed **more than 80 g**; a 330 ml can today weighs about **13 g**
  (International Aluminium Institute) [S21]. Ball Packaging Europe has cut a 330 ml can to just under **9.5 g** [S21]. A
  2014 Aluminum Association release: one can went from 13.34 g to 13.04 g (−2%) [S21].
- So the metal per can has fallen by about 85%. Total can output rose to the order of a hundred billion a year in the US
  alone [approx.; one weak source; verify against the Can Manufacturers Institute before use]. Primary aluminium output
  grew from about 1.5 Mt (1950) to roughly 70 Mt (2020s) [approx.; check IAI].
- The can is the purest lightweighting card: **−85% metal per unit, ×many units**.

### 5.2 Plastics

- **Geyer, Jambeck & Law, *Science Advances* (2017)**: **8,300 million tonnes** of virgin plastics produced to 2015. Of
  c.6,300 Mt of plastic waste generated by 2015, **around 9% was recycled, 12% incinerated, 79% accumulated in landfills
  or the natural environment**. On current trends, about 12,000 Mt in landfills or the environment by 2050 [S22].
  2,500 Mt (30%) still in use [S22].
- Plastic is the "efficiency" material par excellence: lighter packaging cuts transport fuel per unit, while making
  disposable packaging so cheap that the number of units explodes.

### 5.3 Concrete and cement

- **China used c.6.4–6.6 gigatonnes of cement in 2011–2013, more than the c.4.1–4.5 Gt the United States used in the
  whole 20th century** (Smil, *Making the Modern World*, from USGS data; popularised by Bill Gates) [S23]. Figures vary
  by a few tenths with source. Note: **cement**, not concrete.

### 5.4 Textiles: from the spinning jenny to Shein

Efficiency steps (dates standard British industrial history; not individually re-checked in this pass):

| Date | Step |
|---|---|
| 1733 | Kay's flying shuttle (weaving faster than spinning: the first bottleneck) |
| c.1764 (patent 1770) | Hargreaves's spinning jenny (initially 8 spindles per worker) |
| 1769 | Arkwright's water frame; Cromford mill 1771 |
| 1779 | Crompton's spinning mule |
| 1785 | Cartwright's power loom |
| 1825–30 | Roberts's self-acting mule |
| 20th c. | Synthetic fibres (nylon 1935, polyester 1941) |
| 2000s | "Fast fashion" supply chains; 2010s "ultra-fast" online (Shein) |

Total use:
- **Ellen MacArthur Foundation, *A New Textiles Economy* (2017)**: clothing production **approximately doubled in 15
  years** (often given as 2000–2014 or 2000–2015; check the report), while **utilisation — the number of times a garment
  is worn before it is discarded — fell 36%**; in China about 70% [S24]. "One garbage truck of textiles is landfilled or
  burned every second" [S24].
- **Shein**: estimates of new styles added per day vary wildly: c.2,000 SKUs a day (2021, Business of Apps); 2,000–10,000
  (The Hustle, H2 2021); c.3,000 (Euromonitor); c.4,700 designs a day in Q1 2026 per the company's draft Hong Kong
  prospectus (Modaes) [S25]. A widely repeated figure of 314,877 styles in a period (Rest of World) is unverified.
- Mechanism: **cheaper garments are worn fewer times**. The resource per wear rises even as the resource per garment
  falls. That is a lifetime-shrinkage rebound distinct from more-units rebound.

### 5.5 Recycling as moral licence

- **Catlin & Wang, *Journal of Consumer Psychology* 23(1), 2013**, "Recycling gone bad: when the option to recycle
  increases resource consumption": participants testing scissors used more paper when a recycling bin was present than
  when only a rubbish bin was; a field study found paper-towel use in a washroom rose after a recycling bin was added
  [S26]. (The size of the field effect is reported inconsistently in secondary sources — 17% vs c.82%; check the paper.)
- Game use: a Circularity lever that also raises demand by a small percentage ("licence"), so its net effect can be
  negative in an elastic application.

### 5.6 Ironies

- An 80 g can became a 13 g can; the industry celebrates lightweighting in press releases about record volumes.
- 9% of plastic ever discarded was recycled; the triangular recycling symbol is printed on far more than that.

## 6. Housing, space and appliances

### 6.1 Floor area per person (US)

- Median new single-family home: **1,525 sq ft (1973) → 2,467 sq ft (2015)**, c.62% larger; average household size fell
  from c.3.01 to c.2.54 over the same span, so **living space per person in new homes roughly doubled (c.507 → c.987 sq
  ft)** [S27; derived from Census by Mark Perry and FEE; check Census "Characteristics of New Housing"].
- Median new home in 2024: c.2,146 sq ft, c.13% below the 2015 peak [S27; secondary].
- Mechanism: heating and cooling energy per square foot fell (insulation codes, efficient furnaces) while square feet per
  person rose. **Efficiency per m², sprawl per head.**

### 6.2 Refrigerators: the counter-example

- Average new US fridge energy use: **1,941 kWh/yr (1972) → 587 kWh/yr (2002)** (McMahon, ACEEE) and below 500 kWh/yr
  after the 2014 standard [S28]. Between 1947 and 1974 fridge energy use rose from under 400 to over 1,800 kWh/yr [S28].
- **Volume rose, price (in 2010 dollars) fell by about two-thirds, energy fell by more than three-quarters** (ASAP) [S28].
- Why no backfire: one fridge per household is close to saturation (second fridges exist, but demand is near its
  ceiling); standards (California 1978, 1980, 1987; federal 1990, 1993, 2001, 2014) forced the gains [S28].
- This is the game's **saturation lesson made real**: efficiency finally saves resource once an application saturates
  — and standards matter.

### 6.3 Televisions: efficiency eaten by inches

- Average US LCD screen size more than doubled, **23 inches (1998) → 47 inches (2018)** (Statista, citing CTA) [S29].
- LCD TVs in 2015 used **76% less energy per screen area** than in 2003 (CTA) [S29].
- But energy per set rises steeply with size (2023–24: c.88 kWh/yr under 40", c.262 kWh/yr at 60–69", c.338 kWh/yr at
  70"+), and the weighted average energy use of sets sold in 2024–25 rose 8% year on year to c.203 kWh/yr [S29].
- The IEA 4E programme names display size growth as "the major influence on the energy trend" [S29].

## 7. Time-saving, roads, medicine and safety

### 7.1 Housework: Cowan's paradox

- **Joann Vanek (1974)**, using USDA time-use diaries, concluded that full-time housewives' housework time had **not
  declined** over roughly half a century despite appliances [S30].
- **Ruth Schwartz Cowan, *More Work for Mother* (1983)**: appliances reduced drudgery but did not reduce the time spent,
  because standards rose (more laundry, more cleaning, more elaborate cooking) and tasks once shared with servants,
  husbands and children fell to the housewife alone [S30]. Mokyr later called this the "Cowan paradox" (NBER) [S30].
- **Disputed**: Harms & Gershuny (UCL) re-examined recovered diary data and argue Vanek's figures are statistically
  problematic, finding a continuing decline in women's core housework; Vanek's category also bundled childcare,
  shopping and household travel [S30]. A widely repeated "55 hours a week in both the 1920s and 1960s" figure is
  unverified.
- Game mechanic: an application whose **saturation level rises as service gets cheaper** (standards inflation) —
  cleanliness is never "done".
- **The washing machine**: Ha-Joon Chang, *23 Things They Don't Tell You About Capitalism* (2010), argues the washing
  machine changed the world more than the internet; Hans Rosling's TED talk "The magic washing machine" (2010)
  [both standard; not re-checked].
- **Parkinson's law**: C. Northcote Parkinson, *The Economist*, 1955: "Work expands so as to fill the time available
  for its completion" [standard; date 19 Nov 1955 not re-checked].

### 7.2 Roads and travel time: Marchetti's constant

- **Cesare Marchetti, "Anthropological invariants in travel behavior"**, *Technological Forecasting and Social Change*
  47 (1994), pp. 75–88 (IIASA reprint RR-95-04): mean daily travel time is about **one hour** across much of the world;
  Marchetti credits the empirical finding to **Yacov Zahavi** (travel time budget) [S31].
- Zahavi's corollary: faster travel produces longer trips, not saved time [S31].
- **Duranton & Turner, *American Economic Review* (2011), "The fundamental law of road congestion"**: in US metro
  areas, vehicle-kilometres travelled rise roughly one-for-one with interstate highway lane-kilometres; more roads (or
  public transit) are unlikely to relieve congestion [S32].
- UK: the **SACTRA report "Trunk Roads and the Generation of Traffic" (1994)** accepted induced traffic as real [standard
  in UK transport history; not re-checked in this pass].
- Mechanism: **a fixed time budget**. Saved time is re-spent on distance. Every speed upgrade becomes sprawl.

### 7.3 Risk compensation (the Peltzman effect)

- **Sam Peltzman, "The Effects of Automobile Safety Regulation"**, *Journal of Political Economy* 83(4), August 1975,
  pp. 677–725. Engineering studies predicted c.20% more highway deaths without mandated safety devices; Peltzman argued
  drivers offset the devices by driving more intensely, so the offset was "virtually complete": fewer occupant deaths,
  more pedestrian deaths and non-fatal crashes [S33]. Later critiques re-specified the model and found regulation did
  reduce deaths [S33]. **[disputed]**
- **Munich taxi ABS experiment** (Aschenbrenner, Biehl et al.; accident data 1981–83, behaviour 1985–86): taxis randomly
  assigned with and without ABS. ABS cabs were **not** involved in fewer accidents (957 accidents, 91 cabs, 270 with
  ABS); observers recorded more aggressive driving by ABS drivers [S34]. Small sample; results described as mixed in
  places.
- **UK seat belt law** (compulsory for front-seat occupants from 31 January 1983 [date standard; not re-checked]):
  **John Adams** (UCL), *Risk* (1995) and earlier, argued belt laws shifted deaths from occupants to pedestrians and
  cyclists [S35]. **[disputed]**
- **Cycle helmets**: Ian Walker (Bath, 2007) recorded c.2,300–2,500 overtaking vehicles and found drivers passed c.8.5
  cm closer when he wore a helmet; re-analysis (Olivier & Walter 2013) disputes the association [S35]. **[disputed]**
- **Dagen H** (Sweden switched to right-hand traffic, 3 September 1967): crashes fell for a period, then returned to
  trend — risk compensation in reverse [standard; not re-checked].
- Mechanism: **behavioural rebound in safety**. Safety is a resource people "spend" on speed. Directly analogous to
  efficiency being spent on more use.

### 7.4 Antibiotics: efficacy as a depleting commons

- Fleming observed penicillin in 1928. In his **Nobel lecture (11 December 1945)** he warned of under-dosage, in a
  passage widely reproduced as: "The time may come when penicillin can be bought by anyone in the shops. Then there is
  the danger that the ignorant man may easily underdose himself and by exposing his microbes to non-lethal quantities
  of the drug make them resistant." He followed it with a parable of "Mr. X" who has a sore throat [S36]. (I could not
  load the Nobel PDF; the wording is from secondary reproductions. Check before setting in type.)
- Resistance followed quickly: first penicillin resistance observed in the 1940s [S36]; methicillin was introduced in
  1959 and MRSA reported in 1961 [standard; not re-checked].
- **Livestock**: Van Boeckel et al., *PNAS* (2015): global antimicrobial use in food animals projected to rise **67%,
  from 63,151 tonnes (2010) to 105,596 tonnes (2030)**; two-thirds of the rise from more animals; 99% rise in the BRICS
  [S37]. In the US, food animals have been estimated at c.80% of antimicrobial sales [S37]. (A widely cited "73%" global
  share is unverified.)
- Mechanism: cheap, effective antibiotics expanded uses (growth promotion, prophylaxis, intensive farming), and each use
  depletes a shared stock — **efficacy** — that regenerates only through new discovery, which slowed. A pure commons with
  a stock that does not regenerate on its own.

### 7.5 Ironies

- Thomas Midgley: see restraint.md (leaded petrol and CFCs, one inventor).
- Seat belts, ABS and helmets are each credited with making the people around the protected person less safe — none of
  these findings is settled, which is itself the joke.

## 8. Finance

- **Barber & Odean, "Trading Is Hazardous to Your Wealth"**, *Journal of Finance* 55(2), 2000, pp. 773–806: over 60,000
  households at a large discount broker, February 1991 to December 1996. The most active traders earned a
  net annualised geometric mean return of **11.4%**, against **18.5%** for the least active (in the published version)
  [S38]. The authors' conclusion is in their title.
- **Cheaper trading → more trading** (efficiency path; dates standard, not individually re-checked in this pass):
  NYSE fixed commissions abolished on **1 May 1975 ("May Day")**; London's **Big Bang, 27 October 1986**; US
  decimalisation 2001; electronic and high-frequency trading 2000s; **zero-commission** retail trading became standard
  in October 2019 after Schwab, TD Ameritrade and E*Trade matched Robinhood; GameStop squeeze January 2021.
- **Credit**: Bank of America's "Fresno drop" (1958) mailed some 60,000 unsolicited BankAmericards [standard; not
  re-checked].
- Restraint: Sweden's financial transaction tax 1984–1991 (see restraint.md §Leakage).
- Mechanism: the resource is **attention and risk**; cheaper transactions raise volume until net returns fall.

## 9. Waste

- **E-waste**: **62 million tonnes in 2022** (7.8 kg per person), up 82% from 2010; only **22.3%** documented as formally
  collected and recycled; projected to reach 82 Mt by 2030 while the documented rate falls to c.20% (Global E-waste
  Monitor 2024, ITU/UNITAR) [S39]. Europe 42.8%; Africa below 1% [S39]. "E-waste is rising five times faster than
  documented recycling" (UNITAR headline) [S39].
- **Plastics**: see §5.2.
- **Textiles**: see §5.4.
- **UK "fridge mountain" (2002)**: an EU rule on CFC-containing insulation foam came into force before Britain had plants
  to treat it, leaving stockpiles of discarded fridges [standard; size and cost not re-checked]. Headline gold.
- Mechanism: the **sink** is a resource. Bigger bins, cheaper collection and lighter goods raise tonnage; the sink fills.

## 10. Candidates for new eras/sectors, ranked by distinctness of mechanism

Ranked by how different the rebound *mechanism* is from Steam's (more efficiency → cheaper service → more demand for a
stock resource), not by how attractive the setting is.

1. **Fishing (a renewable stock with a collapse threshold, hysteresis and a lying gauge).**
   New mechanics: (a) the stock regenerates logistically, so moderate catch is sustainable for ever; (b) past a threshold
   the regeneration rate itself collapses and stays collapsed for decades (northern cod: 32 years) — a Reckoning that
   leaves a permanent scar; (c) **hyperstability**: the player's catch-per-trip gauge stays green while the stock falls;
   only a Survey Institution reveals the true stock; (d) rivals: foreign fleets fish the same bank until an EEZ lever
   nationalises it — and then the home fleet does the overfishing; (e) every fishing-power upgrade is an efficiency card
   (Thurstan: 17× the power for the same fish). Real arc 1883–1992–2024.

2. **Water (an accounting illusion: applied vs consumed).**
   New mechanic: two gauges — "water applied" (falls with drip) and "water consumed / basin storage" (rises). Efficiency
   cuts the return flow that recharged the aquifer. Subsidies for efficiency raise use (Kansas). A downstream neighbour
   (the basin) pays. Restraint that works is a cap on *depletion*, not on application. Aral Sea and Ogallala as collapse
   states; Kok-Aral as partial recovery.

3. **Safety and medicine (risk compensation; efficacy as a commons).**
   New mechanics: (a) safety upgrades are "spent" by users on speed or risk, so casualties fall less than promised and
   shift to bystanders (Peltzman, Munich taxis, belts, helmets — all disputed, which suits a game about contested
   evidence); (b) antibiotics: each prescription draws down a shared stock (efficacy) that regenerates only through slow
   discovery; agricultural use is the cheapest and largest drain.

4. **Agriculture and land (the sign of the rebound depends on trade).**
   New mechanic: a yield card that spares land in a closed economy and expands it in an open one (Hertel 2014). Plus an
   indirect conversion loss that grows with wealth (36% of crop calories to feed, 12% returned). Plus a sequence of
   fertiliser stocks (guano → nitrate → Haber–Bosch) that each deplete or substitute.

5. **Time (a fixed budget, and standards that rise).**
   New mechanic: the resource is the player's own time; it does not deplete but is **conserved** (Marchetti's one hour):
   every speed-up is re-spent on distance or on higher standards (Cowan). The saturation ceiling of "cleanliness" or
   "reach" rises with cheapness. Distinct because nothing runs out; the reward simply never arrives.

6. **Whaling (sequential depletion down a ladder; substitution saves then a new use kills).**
   New mechanic: a quota in a fungible unit (the Blue Whale Unit) drives depletion species by species, largest first.
   Kerosene removes the lamp-oil application; margarine opens a bigger one. A short, brutal era with a clear restraint
   ending (moratorium) and a defector (scientific whaling).

7. **Materials and fashion (lifetime shrinkage).**
   New mechanic: cheaper units are used fewer times (utilisation −36%), so resource per *use* rises while resource per
   *unit* falls. Recycling with a moral-licence penalty. Lightweighting (can: 80 g → 13 g) as a straight Jevons card.

8. **Housing and appliances (efficiency per m² vs m² per head; the fridge as the counter-example).**
   Useful mainly for showing saturation working (fridges) next to it failing (floor area, TV inches).

9. **Finance (cheaper transactions → volume → worse returns).** Clear mechanism but abstract; better as an event chain
   or a broadsheet sub-plot than an era.

10. **Waste (the sink as a resource).** Better as a Pressure attached to the materials era than as its own era.
